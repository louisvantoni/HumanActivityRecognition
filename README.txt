1.3. Dataset Description

The Elisenbrunnen Audio Dataset consists of urban soundscape recordings collected at different locations around Elisenbrunnen in Aachen, Germany, in May 2015. The recordings capture a variety of environmental sounds, including human activities, natural sounds, and mechanical or traffic-related sounds.

The dataset was originally collected during daytime soundwalks in which audio recordings were made simultaneously with participants' soundscape perception assessments. The recordings have varying durations and are provided in WAV format.

The dataset was subsequently used as test data for deep learning models in the research work entitled "Multilabel Classification of Human Activities in Audio Data of Urban Environments" by Louis Victor Antoni, Margret Sibylle Engel, M. Ercan Altinsoy. For this application, the original recordings can be segmented into six-second audio chunks to facilitate multilabel classification of human activities.

The dataset is intended to support research in environmental sound analysis, urban soundscape assessment, audio-based human activity recognition, and machine learning.

4. METHODOLOGICAL INFORMATION

4.1. Description of Methods Used for Collection/Generation of Data

The audio recordings were collected at Elisenbrunnen in Aachen, Germany, during daytime soundwalks conducted on 12, 13, and 18 May 2015. The recordings were acquired simultaneously with participants' soundscape perception assessments.

The microphone was positioned approximately 1.5 m above ground level, away from nearby reflecting surfaces.

Microphone calibration was performed using a Brüel & Kjær (B&K) Type 4231 sound calibrator. The recording device was additionally calibrated using a voltage calibrator (0.01 V).

The audio recordings were acquired at a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz.

Calibration files are not included in the dataset but can be requested from the dataset owner, subject to availability.

4.2. Instrumentation

The following equipment was used for audio data acquisition:

Recording device: ZOOM H6 multitrack audio recorder.
Microphone: Sennheiser KE 4 omnidirectional microphone capsule.
Microphone positioning: Approximately 1.5 m above ground level.
Sampling rate: 44.1 kHz.
Calibration equipment: Brüel & Kjær Type 4231 sound calibrator and voltage calibrator (0.01 V).

4.3. Methods for Processing the Data

The original audio recordings have varying durations. For their use in multilabel human activity classification, the recordings can be divided into fixed-length audio segments of six seconds.

A Python script named util.py is provided to facilitate this process. It contains the function split_audio_into_chunks(), which takes the input directory containing the original audio files and the output directory where the segmented audio files will be stored.

The function can be used to generate six-second audio segments from the original recordings. The resulting segments can subsequently be used as input for audio classification and machine learning applications.

The original recordings are provided without segmentation to preserve the complete audio data and allow researchers to apply alternative segmentation strategies.

5. DATA QUALITY AND LIMITATIONS
5.1. Data Quality

The dataset consists of original urban soundscape recordings collected under real-world environmental conditions.

The recordings have varying durations and may contain overlapping sound sources, background noise, and other environmental interference.

The original audio files are provided in WAV format with a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz.


5.2. Limitations of the Dataset

The dataset was collected at a limited number of urban locations in Aachen, Germany, during a specific period in May 2015. Consequently, the recordings may not represent the full diversity of urban soundscapes, geographical environments, seasons, or weather conditions.

The recordings have varying durations and were collected under real-world environmental conditions. Background noise and overlapping sound sources may affect the identification and classification of individual activities.

The dataset should therefore be considered a location- and context-specific collection of urban soundscape recordings rather than a comprehensive representation of urban environments.
